What good looks like
  • Starts on time, under 15 minutes
  • Every miss gets an owner before the team leaves
  • The board is updated, not just discussed
✦ Recommended template
Daily Tier Review Agenda →
How to run it
  1. Gather at the board at the same time each day; the team lead opens.
  2. Read the SQDC metrics left to right; call out any miss against standard.
  3. Each miss gets one owner and one due date, captured live on the action log.
  4. Escalate anything that cannot close today to the weekly review owner.
  5. Close in under 15 minutes; update the board before walking away.
Worked example
Cell daily stand-up · Lynn, MA
A cell holds a 10-minute SQDC stand-up each morning at 7:00. The team lead reads the board. Two misses from yesterday get owners before the team disperses. The board reflects actuals, not plan.
Who owns it
The team lead owns the daily review. The value-stream manager spot-checks adherence weekly.

The weekly operating review.

For managers connecting the daily boards to weekly goals. Weekly
Recommended template
Weekly Operating Review
Tier 2 · value-stream level
What good looks like
  • Reviews the metrics that moved, not all metrics
  • Closes or escalates open actions before adding new ones
  • Ends with the week set: priorities named, owners confirmed
✦ Recommended template
Weekly Operating Review →
How to run it
  1. Open the action log from last week; close or escalate before new business.
  2. Review SQDC trend lines, not point-in-time snapshots.
  3. Identify the two or three items that need a decision or resource this week.
  4. Assign owners and due dates for anything that moves to next week.
  5. Close by naming the top priority for the value stream this week.
Worked example
Value-stream weekly review · Evendale, OH
A value-stream manager runs a 45-minute weekly review off the tier boards every Monday at 8:00. Of ten open actions from last week, eight close in the meeting. Two escalate to the site leader with a clear ask.
Who owns it
The value-stream manager. Cell leads attend and own their tier's metrics.

The monthly business review.

For leaders steering the value stream against the plan. Monthly
Recommended template
Monthly Business Review
Tier 3 · site/business-unit level
What good looks like
  • Trends, not snapshots: three months minimum on every chart
  • Decisions are made and recorded before the meeting ends
  • The cadence connects to strategy, not just execution
✦ Recommended template
Monthly Business Review →
How to run it
  1. Start with a 90-day trend view of SQDC; flag any break from the trajectory.
  2. Review escalations that surfaced from weekly reviews this month.
  3. Make decisions on anything in the escalation log; record the decision live.
  4. Confirm resource or priority adjustments needed before next month.
  5. Close by connecting the month's performance to the annual plan gap.
Worked example
Site monthly business review · Durham, NC
A site leader runs a 90-minute monthly review tying three-month SQDC trends to the annual plan. Four escalations from weekly reviews are resolved in the meeting. Two resource requests are approved before the session ends.
Who owns it
The site leader or business-unit leader. Value-stream managers present their tier's performance.

01

Pick the cadence and tier

Before anything is scheduled, decide which cadence tier you are standing up and who owns it. A daily tier-1 review lives at the cell level. A weekly tier-2 review sits at the value stream. A monthly tier-3 review belongs to the site or business unit. Pick one cadence to start, not all three at once.

Worked exampleA value-stream manager identifies that the team has no consistent weekly review. Metrics are discussed ad hoc, actions fall through. She decides to stand up a tier-2 weekly operating review first. Daily stand-ups are already running. Monthly is handled at the site level. One tier, one owner.
5 minutes Cadence Map
  • Name the cadence tier: daily, weekly, or monthly
  • Name the single owner for this review right now
  • Confirm which team or value stream this cadence covers

02

Set the standing agenda

A standing agenda means the review follows the same structure every time. Attendees arrive knowing the order. The owner opens, the board drives the discussion, the action log closes. Aim for a draft in the template, not a final. You will tighten it after the first run.

Worked exampleThe value-stream manager opens the Weekly Operating Review template and drafts the standing agenda: 5 minutes on open actions, 20 minutes on SQDC metrics by cell, 15 minutes on escalations and decisions, 5 minutes to close with the week's top priority. Eight minutes to draft. She shares it with cell leads before Monday.
8 minutes Review Agenda
  • Set the time box for the review and protect it on the calendar
  • Draft the standing agenda with named sections and time allocations
  • Confirm the action log is open and visible during the review
Open the Review Agenda Template
03

Run it on time

Protect the time. Start even if the room is not full. The owner calls out misses against the board, not opinions. Every action that leaves the meeting has one owner and one due date. Nothing enters the action log without both. The test: could an observer tell which actions are open and who owns each one?

Worked exampleThe first weekly review runs 45 minutes. The manager opens at 8:00 regardless of who is seated. She reads the board, not the deck. Eight of ten open actions from the prior week close in the meeting. Two are escalated with a named ask. The action log is updated before the meeting ends, not after.
3 minutes to set up Action Log
  • Start on time; the agenda drives the meeting, not attendance
  • Every action leaving the meeting has one owner and one due date
  • Update the action log before the room empties
Open the Action Log Template
04

Close the loop

Actions without follow-up erode trust in the cadence faster than anything else. Before the next review, any action that slips its due date gets escalated to the next tier. The escalation path is defined before you need it. After three to four cycles, audit the agenda itself: what is not adding value, what is missing.

Worked exampleAfter four weeks, the value-stream manager reviews the action log. Eight of ten actions per week are closing on time. Two recurring slips involve a supplier response. She escalates both to the monthly business review with a clear ask. After six weeks she trims the weekly agenda by ten minutes because two sections were not driving decisions.
2 minutes per cycle Escalation Path
  • Define the escalation path before the first review runs
  • Review open actions before adding new ones at each session
  • Audit the agenda after four cycles; remove what is not driving decisions

FAQ

Common questions

How long should a daily review be? +

Fifteen minutes is the target. The standing rule is: if it runs past 15 minutes, something is being solved in the review instead of assigned. The review surfaces problems and assigns owners. Solving happens offline. If your daily review is running 30 minutes consistently, the agenda has drifted from its purpose. Audit it, cut the section that is becoming a problem-solving session, and assign that work outside the cadence.

What is the difference between a tier 1 and a tier 3 review? +

Tier 1 is the team-level daily review at the board. It covers the work that happened yesterday and today. Tier 2 is the value-stream weekly review that aggregates the tier-1 results and drives decisions at the value-stream level. Tier 3 is the site or business-unit monthly review that connects the weekly results to the annual plan. Problems that cannot be solved at tier 1 escalate to tier 2. Problems that cannot be solved at tier 2 escalate to tier 3. Each tier has a different owner, a different time horizon, and a different purpose.

What goes on the agenda? +

For a daily review: SQDC metrics, misses against standard, and action assignment. For a weekly review: open actions from last week, SQDC trend review by cell, escalations that need a decision this week, and one top priority for the value stream. For a monthly review: 90-day SQDC trends, escalation resolution, resource and priority decisions, and the connection to the annual plan. The standing rule is: anything on the agenda that is not driving a decision or surfacing a problem should be removed. The agenda is not a status report. It is the operating system for the team.

How do actions get closed? +

An action closes when the owner brings proof of completion to the next review. Proof is specific: a changed process, a resolved defect, a decision recorded. "Worked on it" is not a close. If an action cannot close by its due date, the owner escalates it before the next review with a clear ask: what decision or resource is needed to unblock it. Actions that slip without escalation signal that the cadence is not being held. The discipline of closing actions is what separates a structured operating cadence from a status meeting.

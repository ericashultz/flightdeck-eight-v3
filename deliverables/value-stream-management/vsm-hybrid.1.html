A production value stream.

For flow from raw material to finished product Manufacturing
Recommended template
Manufacturing VSM
Door-to-door production flow
What good looks like
  • Every step timed, inventory and lead time visible
  • The bottleneck is obvious and named
  • Value-add time vs. total lead time is measured
✦ Recommended template
Manufacturing VSM →
How to map it
  1. Walk the flow door to door. Start at shipping and work back to raw material.
  2. Record every process step, cycle time, changeover, and uptime.
  3. Capture inventory counts and lead times between every step.
  4. Draw the information flow: what triggers each step and who sends the signal.
  5. Calculate total lead time and value-add time. The gap is your opportunity.
Worked example
Turbine blade line · Lynn, MA
A six-step blade line maps door to door and finds the grind cell is the constraint. Total lead time is 14 days. Value-add time is 38 minutes. The grind queue holds 4 days of inventory. The map makes the target obvious: cut grind queue first.
Who owns it
Value stream manager or the site lean leader. Not a project manager in a conference room: the map is drawn on the floor by the people who run the flow.

An office or service value stream.

For information flows like quoting, closing, or onboarding Transactional
Recommended template
Transactional VSM
Office and information flow
What good looks like
  • Hand-offs and wait time shown between every step
  • Rework loops named and measured
  • The slow step is clear and owned
✦ Recommended template
Transactional VSM →
How to map it
  1. Define the trigger event and the output that ends the stream.
  2. Walk the process with the people who run it. Follow one request end to end.
  3. Record each step, its owner, and the time to complete vs. the wait before it starts.
  4. Identify rework loops: where does work come back, and why?
  5. Calculate percent value-add. Most transactional streams are under 20%.
Worked example
Quote-to-cash stream · Services
A quote-to-cash stream maps 11 steps across Sales, Contracts, Finance, and Ops. Total lead time is 14 days. Value-add time is 6 hours. Nine of those 14 days are waiting, mostly in Contracts review. The map makes the constraint visible in one page.
Who owns it
The process owner for the stream, not the functional leader of each silo. For quote-to-cash, the Commercial Operations director.

A design or development value stream.

For engineering flows from request to released design Engineering
Recommended template
Engineering VSM
Design request to released drawing
What good looks like
  • Gates and reviews mapped with queue time at each
  • Rework driver identified and measured
  • Redundant approvals surfaced and named
✦ Recommended template
Engineering VSM →
How to map it
  1. Define the trigger (design request) and the exit (released drawing or approved design).
  2. Map every step: analysis, design, review gate, revision, approval, release.
  3. Capture time at each gate: active work time and queue time waiting for review.
  4. Trace where designs come back: which reviews drive the most rework loops?
  5. Count approvals. Two engineers signing off on the same checkpoint is a redundancy signal.
Worked example
Design release stream · GE9X parts
A design release stream maps from change request to released drawing across eight steps. Total lead time is 22 days. Active design time is 4 days. The map surfaces two redundant approval steps adding 6 days of queue. Removing them cuts cycle time by 27% without touching the actual engineering work.
Who owns it
Chief engineer or design systems lead for the product. The people who control the gate sequence own the map.
Guidance when needed

Map your value stream in four moves.

Four moves take you from a blank page to a working current-state map and a clear future-state target. Each move has a short action, a worked example, the template it points to, and a small checklist. Check items off as you go.

Scoped to all stream types. Pick a stream above to tailor every example.
Every VSM reveals three things: Where value is created Where time is lost What to fix first
Your progress
0 of 12 tasks complete
01

Map the current state

Walk the flow and record every step, the time it takes, and the inventory or work in queue between steps. Do not map what should happen. Map what actually happens today, including the detours, waits, and rework loops.

Worked exampleA manufacturing team walks the turbine blade line door to door. They record six process steps, cycle times ranging from 4 to 38 minutes, and inventory queues between each step. The grind cell has 4 days of inventory in front of it. Total lead time: 14 days. Value-add time: 38 minutes. That ratio is the opening finding.
10 minutes Current-State Map
  • Walk or trace the flow end to end before drawing anything
  • Record cycle time, wait time, and queue size at every step
  • Calculate total lead time and total value-add time. Note the ratio.
Open the Current-State Map Template
02

Find the waste

With the current-state map drawn, mark every step that adds no value from the customer's perspective. Look specifically for wait time between steps, rework loops, overproduction (more than the next step needs), and unnecessary hand-offs. Then find the constraint: the one step that limits the whole stream.

Worked exampleThe blade team marks the grind queue (4 days), a quality rework loop that returns 12% of parts to grinding, and a batch scheduling trigger that creates unnecessary overproduction at the forge step. The constraint is grind: it runs at 94% utilization while every other step runs under 60%. All three waste items point to the same bottleneck.
8 minutes Waste Walk
  • Mark every wait and queue on the map with a waste burst symbol
  • Identify rework loops and trace them to their source step
  • Name the constraint: the one step that, if improved, most improves the whole stream
Open the Waste Walk Template
03

Design the future state

Draw the flow you want, removing the biggest waste first. Do not try to eliminate everything at once. A future state that attacks the constraint and removes the largest single waste is better than an ambitious redesign that never gets implemented. The future state is a target, not a wish list.

Worked exampleThe blade team draws a future state that: adds one additional grind shift to relieve the constraint, replaces batch scheduling at forge with a pull signal tied to grind completion, and adds an in-process quality check before the grind step to cut the 12% rework rate. Projected lead time drops from 14 days to 6 days. Value-add percentage climbs from 0.2% to 0.6%. Still low, but the constraint is addressed and the improvement is implementable in 90 days.
8 minutes Future-State Map
  • Draw the future state targeting the constraint first, not every waste at once
  • Set a target lead time and target value-add percentage for the future state
  • Confirm the future state is achievable in 60 to 90 days, not a 3-year vision
Open the Future-State Map Template
04

Plan and manage to it

Break the future state into owned, dated improvements and review them on a cadence. The map without a plan is a decoration. Each gap between current and future state becomes a kaizen burst: a named owner, a target date, and a measurable result. Review progress monthly at minimum.

Worked exampleThe blade team creates three kaizen bursts from the future state: (1) Add grind shift, owner: site operations manager, target: 30 days; (2) Implement pull signal at forge, owner: production control lead, target: 45 days; (3) Add in-process quality gate, owner: quality engineer, target: 60 days. The value-stream manager reviews status at the monthly operating review and posts the current-vs-future map on the tier board so the whole team sees where it stands.
5 minutes VSM Action Plan
  • Convert every gap into a kaizen burst with a named owner and target date
  • Schedule a monthly review of the action plan against the future state
  • Post the current-vs-future map where the team can see progress daily

FAQ

Common questions

What is the difference between value-add and non-value-add time? +

Value-add time is any step the customer would pay for if they knew about it: forming a part, writing a design, completing an analysis. Non-value-add time is everything else: waiting in a queue, being batched, moving between buildings, sitting in an inbox. Most processes are under 10% value-add when mapped honestly. That number is not a failure; it is the opportunity. The goal of VSM is to make the ratio visible so you know where to improve.

How detailed should the map be? +

Detailed enough to see the constraint and the largest waste. A value stream map is not a process map of every micro-step. It operates at the level of major process boxes, not individual tasks within a step. If you find yourself drawing twenty boxes for a single manufacturing cell, you have gone too deep. A first map should fit on one large sheet of paper and take one focused session to draw. Go deeper only if the analysis requires it.

Current state or future state first? +

Current state first, always. A future state drawn without a current-state map is a solution looking for a problem. You cannot know what to fix until you see what is actually happening. Walk the flow, draw it as it runs today, quantify the waste, name the constraint, and only then draw the future state. Teams that skip the current state tend to improve the wrong step or set targets with no basis in the data.

Who should be in the mapping session? +

The people who run the flow, not the people who manage it. A mapping session with only managers produces a map of what managers think happens. You need the operator who runs the grind cell, the analyst who processes the contract, the engineer who owns the review gate. Aim for a small cross-functional group (four to eight people) who collectively touch every step. Managers set the scope and attend the out-brief; the people who do the work draw the map.

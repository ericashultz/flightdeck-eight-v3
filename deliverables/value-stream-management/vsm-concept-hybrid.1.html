Create a Value Stream Map

Create a Value Stream Map, step by step

01

Prepare (before the event)

Define the value stream and its boundaries

Name the start point, the end point, and the customer. Scope one end-to-end value stream — not the whole enterprise. A macro-level stream mapped in a five-day event produces a map too abstract to act on.

Select the product or process family

Use PQPR — Product, Quantity, Process, Routing. A family exists where 80–90% of process steps and routing are shared.

Walk the process before deciding; the matrix organizes what you observe, it does not replace observing.
Calculate takt time and gather baseline data

Collect cycle times, changeover, uptime, first-pass yield and inventory before the event begins.

Takt Time = Net Available Time ÷ Customer Demand.
Form the team and name the value stream owner

Cross-functional, covering every function the flow crosses, plus the people who do the work daily.

Name the owner before the event starts — an owner named during the event, or after it, does not own what the team designed.
02

See the current state (at genba)

Walk and map material and information flow

Process boxes left to right with a data box beneath each. Information flow above, right to left. Map how the process actually runs, not how it is documented to run.

Add inventory and queues

Count by hand at genba and note the time of day — a queue looks different at shift start than shift end. Add push arrows wherever a process produces to a schedule rather than to downstream demand.

Draw the timeline ladder

Valleys are process time, peaks are queue time.

Lead Time = peaks + valleys. Flow Efficiency = valleys ÷ lead time × 100%.
This is where the flow metrics come from — the ladder is not decoration.
03

Design the ideal state

Sketch the ideal state before the future state

Zero waste, zero constraints — what you would build with no budget, resource or feasibility limit. A short exercise, before the seven guidelines are applied.

This sets the ceiling first, so the achievable future state does not quietly settle for whatever is easiest to implement.
Do not roadmap to the ideal state

The ideal state is a reference point, not a target condition. The roadmap is built against the achievable future state designed in phase 4.

04

Design the future state: the seven guidelines, in order

These are sequential and interdependent, not a menu — each depends on the ones before it.

1
Produce to takt time

Apply the takt calculated in preparation. Where cycle time exceeds takt, that process is the constraint and must be fixed first. Where cycle time sits well below takt, consider combining that step with an adjacent one.

2
Build continuous flow wherever possible

Combine, rearrange, simplify. One piece moving from process to process with no queue between them. This is where the largest lead-time reductions come from.

3
Use a supermarket to pull where you cannot flow

If you cannot flow, pull. A supermarket is a sized inventory the downstream process draws from and the upstream process replenishes on a kanban signal. Size it to replenishment lead time plus a small buffer. Where variety is low and lead time short, a FIFO lane is simpler and sufficient.

4
Send the customer schedule to only one point

Choose the pacemaker. Everything upstream flows or pulls in response to what the pacemaker consumed; everything downstream flows directly to the customer.

Scheduling every process independently is the signature of a push system.
5
Level the mix

Interleave product variants at the pacemaker rather than running large batches of each. Batching forces every upstream process to guess at demand for days at a time.

6
Level the volume

Release and withdraw small, consistent increments. A sized breakwater buffer just ahead of the pacemaker absorbs small swings in customer demand.

Pitch = Takt Time × Pack-Out Quantity.
7
Develop the capability to make every part every interval

EPEI is the time between runs of the same variant, and it is the capability check behind the other six — without it, level loading and small-batch flow stay theoretical. EPEI is a manufacturing measure; in transactional work use handle time, queue time and flow efficiency instead.

05

Build the transformation roadmap

Grasp the situation

Quantify the gap between current-state and future-state performance. One metric gap, sized in hard numbers — not several.

Analyze root cause

Build a Pareto of where the gap comes from, ranked largest to smallest. This is what forces the team onto the biggest driver rather than the loudest complaint.

Set countermeasures and plan

One fix per Pareto driver, sequenced by month, each with one named owner and a real date. Classify by effort: just-do-it, standard work update, workshop, kaizen event, or project.

Check and sustain

Verify the metric moved and the result held.

06

Execute and sustain

Execute the roadmap month by month

Track countermeasure completion against the metric gap, not against activity counts.

Hand the result into daily management

New standard work, new targets, new visual controls. An improvement that never updates daily management delivers a one-time result and then drifts back.

Re-map when the roadmap is complete

The future state designed this year becomes the current state mapped next year.

Common questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between value-add and non-value-add time? +

Value-add time is any step the customer would pay for if they knew about it: forming a part, writing a design, completing an analysis. Non-value-add time is everything else: waiting in a queue, being batched, moving between buildings, sitting in an inbox. Most processes are under 10% value-add when mapped honestly. That number is not a failure; it is the opportunity. The goal of VSM is to make the ratio visible so you know where to improve.

How detailed should the map be? +

Detailed enough to see the constraint and the largest waste. A value stream map is not a process map of every micro-step. It operates at the level of major process boxes, not individual tasks within a step. If you find yourself drawing twenty boxes for a single manufacturing cell, you have gone too deep. A first map should fit on one large sheet of paper and take one focused session to draw. Go deeper only if the analysis requires it.

Current state or future state first? +

Current state first, always. A future state drawn without a current-state map is a solution looking for a problem. You cannot know what to fix until you see what is actually happening. Walk the flow, draw it as it runs today, quantify the waste, name the constraint, and only then draw the future state. Teams that skip the current state tend to improve the wrong step or set targets with no basis in the data.

Who should be in the mapping session? +

The people who run the flow, not the people who manage it. A mapping session with only managers produces a map of what managers think happens. You need the operator who runs the grind cell, the analyst who processes the contract, the engineer who owns the review gate. Aim for a small cross-functional group (four to eight people) who collectively touch every step. Managers set the scope and attend the out-brief; the people who do the work draw the map.

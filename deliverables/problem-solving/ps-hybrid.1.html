Just-do-it problems.

For obvious problems with a known cause and a fast fix. Quick fix
Recommended template
Quick Kaizen / Just-Do-It card
Fix it at the source, share it so it does not recur
What good looks like
  • Fixed at the source, not patched at the symptom
  • Shared with the team so it does not recur
  • Took minutes, not meetings
✦ Recommended template
Quick Kaizen / Just-Do-It card →
How to apply it
  1. Confirm the cause is obvious and within your control to fix today.
  2. Fix it at the point of origin, not at the next step downstream.
  3. Update the standard or label to prevent recurrence.
  4. Fill in a Just-Do-It card: what was wrong, what you did, who needs to know.
  5. Share it at the next team huddle or post it on the visual board.
Worked example
Mislabeled bin on the assembly line
A frontline operator notices a bin labeled M6 contains M8 fasteners. The cause is clear: the wrong bag was restocked. The operator fixes the bin, updates the label, adds a visual check to the restocking standard, and logs a Just-Do-It card. Done in one shift.
Who owns it
The person who finds it. Escalate only if the fix requires authority or budget you do not have.

One clear problem, one page.

For recurring problems that need root-cause analysis. Structured (A3)
Recommended template
A3 Problem Solving Template
Sharp problem statement, root cause found not guessed
What good looks like
  • A sharp problem statement: what, where, when, how big
  • Root cause found, not guessed
  • Countermeasure verified against the target condition
✦ Recommended template
A3 Problem Solving Template →
How to apply it
  1. Write a crisp problem statement: what is happening, where, when, how big the gap.
  2. Set the target condition: what does good look like when this is solved?
  3. Run 5 Whys or a fishbone to reach the root cause, not just the symptom.
  4. Choose a countermeasure that addresses the cause, with an owner and a date.
  5. Verify the fix: measure the effect against the target over two or more cycles.
Worked example
Repeating torque defects in a manufacturing cell
A cell runs an A3 on a torque defect that spiked from 2 to 9 per week. Five Whys traces the cause to fixture wear, not operator technique. The team replaces the fixture and adds a wear check to the standard. Defects drop to 1 per week over the verification period.
Who owns it
Process owner or cell lead, supported by a manufacturing engineer. The A3 is a thinking tool, not a report.

Cross-functional, data-heavy problems.

For complex problems spanning teams, owned by a Black Belt or process owner. Systemic (DMAIC)
Recommended template
DMAIC Project Charter
Defined scope, measured baseline, validated root cause
What good looks like
  • Defined scope with a business case and a baseline metric
  • Validated root cause supported by data, not opinion
  • Controlled result with a control plan that sustains the gain
✦ Recommended template
DMAIC Project Charter →
How to apply it
  1. Define: scope the problem, set the goal, identify the team and sponsor.
  2. Measure: baseline the process with data; map variation sources.
  3. Analyze: use statistical tools to validate the root cause.
  4. Improve: design and pilot the solution; confirm the gain against the baseline.
  5. Control: build a control plan and hand off to the process owner for sustain.
Worked example
Multi-site yield problem
A yield gap spanning three manufacturing sites is escalated to a Black Belt. The DMAIC project baselines yield across sites, isolates a material variation root cause through regression analysis, redesigns the incoming inspection threshold, and closes with a control plan reviewed quarterly by the process owner.
Who owns it
Black Belt or Green Belt, sponsored by a site or value-stream leader. Not appropriate for a single operator to run solo.
Guidance when needed

Your first A3 in four moves.

Four moves take you from a problem you cannot name to a verified countermeasure. Each move has a short action, a worked example, the template it points to, and a small checklist. Check items off as you go.

Scoped to all methods. Pick a method above to tailor every example.
Every A3 answers three questions: What is the real problem? What caused it? Did the fix work?
Your progress
0 of 12 tasks complete
01

Define the problem

Write a problem statement that captures what is happening, where it happens, when it started, and how big the gap is between current state and target. A sharp problem statement prevents solving the wrong thing. Resist the urge to jump to a cause before the problem is named clearly.

Worked exampleTorque defects on line 3 rose from 2 to 9 per week over the last month. Target is 2 or fewer per week. The defects appear only on the M6 bolt sequence during final close, not during sub-assembly.
5 minutes A3 Template
  • Write the problem statement: what, where, when, how big
  • State the current condition and the target condition
  • Confirm you are describing a symptom, not already assuming a cause
Open the A3 Template
02

Find the root cause

Run 5 Whys or build a fishbone diagram to separate the symptom from the cause. Ask why the problem occurs, then ask why that condition exists, and keep going until you reach a cause you can actually change. Stop when one more "why" goes outside the scope of the problem or the team.

Worked exampleWhy defects? Torque out of spec. Why out of spec? Wrench not reaching the required setting. Why? Fixture holding the part has excessive wear that shifts the bolt angle. Why worn? No wear-check interval in the standard. Root cause: missing preventive inspection in the standard, not operator error.
10 minutes 5 Whys Worksheet
  • Ask why at least five times before settling on a root cause
  • Verify the cause chain: if you fix the root cause, does the problem go away?
  • Confirm the root cause is actionable within your scope
Open the 5 Whys Worksheet
03

Choose a countermeasure

Pick the fix that addresses the root cause directly. Assign an owner and a date before leaving this step. A countermeasure without both is a wish, not a plan. If multiple options exist, pick the one with the clearest line to the root cause, not the easiest one to implement.

Worked exampleCountermeasure: replace the worn fixture and add a fixture wear check to the leader standard work, every 50 cycles. Owner: cell lead. Due: end of week. Interim containment: manual torque verification on every unit until the new fixture is installed.
5 minutes Countermeasure Plan
  • The countermeasure addresses the root cause, not the symptom
  • An owner and a completion date are assigned
  • Containment is in place while the fix is being implemented
Open the Countermeasure Plan
04

Confirm and sustain

Verify the effect of the countermeasure against the target condition. Measure over at least two cycles before declaring success. If the effect is confirmed, standardize and add to leader standard work. If not, return to step 2 and re-examine the root cause.

Worked exampleOver two weeks post-fix, defects drop from 9 per week to 1. The fixture wear check is added to the cell lead daily routine. The A3 is shared at the weekly problem-solving review. The improvement is documented and the standard is updated.
3 minutes to set up PDCA Follow-up
  • Verify the effect over at least two cycles against the target condition
  • Standardize the fix and update the relevant standard or leader standard work
  • Share the A3 so others can learn from the root cause, not just the fix

FAQ

Common questions

When do I use an A3 vs just fixing it? +

Fix it if you already know the cause and the fix is in your hands right now. Use an A3 when the problem recurs, when you are not sure of the cause, or when the fix requires coordination across more than one person or shift. The test: if you fixed a version of this problem before and it came back, you need an A3.

What if I cannot find the root cause? +

First, check that your problem statement is specific enough. Vague problems produce vague causes. If the statement is sharp and you are still stuck, try switching methods: move from 5 Whys to a fishbone diagram to expose categories of causes you have not explored. Ask Wingmate to push back on each "why" answer you have written so far. If the cause genuinely requires data across multiple cycles or sites to confirm, that is a signal to escalate to a DMAIC approach.

How is this different from a corrective action? +

A corrective action in a quality management system (CAR, CAPA) is a formal record of a fix required by a customer, audit, or regulatory requirement. An A3 is a thinking tool you use to reach the right fix before you document it. The A3 informs the corrective action; it is not a substitute for it. In practice, the A3 often becomes the evidence package that supports the CAR.

Who should be in the room? +

For a Quick fix: the person closest to the work, no meeting required. For an A3: the process owner and one or two people who see the problem directly, ideally from different shifts or roles. Avoid large groups at the root-cause step. For DMAIC: a defined cross-functional team with a sponsor, a Black Belt or Green Belt, and representatives from each affected area. The right people are those who can observe the problem and implement the fix, not those who need to be informed of it.

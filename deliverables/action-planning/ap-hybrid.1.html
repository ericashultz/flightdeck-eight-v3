Pick your plan type

Start with what you are planning.

Three plan types cover the vast majority of action planning at GE Aerospace. Pick the one that matches your situation to see the right template, worked example, and what good looks like.

A kaizen or A3 follow-through.

For turning a solved problem into sustained change Improvement
Recommended template
Kaizen Action Plan
Post-kaizen or A3 countermeasure tracking
What good looks like
  • Every countermeasure has an owner and a date
  • Progress is visible to the team on the board
  • Results are verified, not assumed
✦ Recommended template
Kaizen Action Plan →
How to use it
  1. Pull every countermeasure off the A3 and list it as a discrete action.
  2. Assign one named owner to each action, not a team or a function.
  3. Set a due date for each action, not a range.
  4. Post the plan on the board where the team meets daily.
  5. Review status at the tier meeting; close verified items, escalate blocked ones.
Worked example
A3 close-out · Cell yield improvement
A cell turns an A3 into six owned actions tracked on the tier board. Four are closed in two weeks. One is escalated when the supplier misses a date. The sixth triggers a standard work update that sticks.
Who owns it
The kaizen team lead. For A3 follow-through, the problem owner named on the A3 header.

A multi-week initiative.

For cross-functional work with milestones and dependencies Project
Recommended template
Project Action Plan
Multi-week initiative with named milestones
What good looks like
  • Milestones and owners are clear and named
  • Dependencies are surfaced before they become blockers
  • Status is honest, not optimistic
✦ Recommended template
Project Action Plan →
How to use it
  1. Define the outcome and the target date before listing any actions.
  2. Break the work into milestones; each milestone has one owner.
  3. Name the dependencies between milestones explicitly.
  4. Surface risks at the start, not after they land.
  5. Review status weekly; update the plan in the meeting, not after it.
Worked example
Line move · Engine final assembly
A line move is planned as 12 milestones with named owners and a weekly status review. Two dependencies are flagged in week one and resolved before they become blockers. The move completes on schedule.
Who owns it
The project lead. For line moves, the manufacturing engineering manager with operations support.

The daily action follow-up.

For the small actions that come off the tier board every day Daily
Recommended template
Daily Action Log
Stand-up captures and same-day close-out
What good looks like
  • Actions captured live, not reconstructed after the meeting
  • Owners named in the room, not assigned by email later
  • Closed or escalated by the next stand-up
✦ Recommended template
Daily Action Log →
How to use it
  1. Open the log before the stand-up starts, not after.
  2. Capture each action as it is called out: what, who, when.
  3. Do not leave the meeting without an owner and a date for every item.
  4. Review open items first at the next stand-up.
  5. Escalate anything open more than two days to the tier above.
Worked example
Morning tier-1 stand-up · Assembly cell
A stand-up generates four actions. Three are closed by the following morning. One is escalated at day two when the parts request stalls. The log is the record, not someone's memory.
Who owns it
The tier facilitator captures. Each action owner reports at the next stand-up.
Guidance when needed

Build an action plan in four moves.

Four moves take you from a goal or a solved problem to an owned, tracked action plan. Each move has a short task, a worked example, the right template, and a small checklist. Check items off as you go.

Scoped to all plan types. Pick a plan type above to tailor every example.
Every action needs three things: What: the concrete step Who: one named owner When: one specific date
Your progress
0 of 12 tasks complete
01

Name the outcome

Write the result you want in one sentence, measurable and dated. An action plan without a clear outcome is a list of tasks, not a plan. Everything else flows from this.

Worked exampleA kaizen team writes: "Reduce first-pass yield loss on line 4 from 3.2% to 1.5% by July 31." Measurable, dated, owned by the team lead. The A3 countermeasures become the action list in step 2.
4 minutes Plan Charter
  • Write the outcome in one sentence: what changes, by how much, by when
  • Confirm the outcome is measurable, not directional (not "improve yield," but "yield from 3.2% to 1.5%")
  • Name the single person accountable for the outcome
Open the Plan Charter Template
02

Break it into actions

List the concrete steps that will get you to the outcome. Each action should be specific enough that you know exactly when it is done. Avoid bundled actions: if a step has two verbs, split it.

Worked exampleThe kaizen team pulls six countermeasures off their A3: update the setup checklist, retrain two operators on the seating sequence, order new fixture blocks, revise the inspection gate, post the updated standard at the station, and verify yield after two weeks. Six discrete actions, each with a clear completion signal.
8 minutes Action List
  • List every action needed to reach the outcome (verb first: Update, Retrain, Order, Verify)
  • Split any action that has two verbs or two deliverables
  • Confirm each action has a clear done signal you can check off
Open the Action List Template
03

Assign owners and dates

One name and one date per action. Shared ownership means no ownership. A date range means no date. This is the single most common failure point in action planning: teams skip this step or leave it vague.

Worked exampleEach of the six kaizen actions gets a named owner (not "cell team") and a specific due date (not "end of month"). The fixture block order goes to the supply chain coordinator with a July 8 date. The operator retraining goes to the cell lead with a July 10 date. No ambiguity.
5 minutes Owner & Date Grid
  • Every action has exactly one owner (a name, not a team or a title)
  • Every action has exactly one due date (a calendar date, not a week or a sprint)
  • Every owner has confirmed they accept the action and the date
Open the Owner & Date Grid
04

Track and close

Make status visible and review it on a cadence. An action plan that is not reviewed is a wish list. Review at the right tier: daily stand-up for same-week actions, weekly review for longer projects.

Worked exampleThe six-action kaizen plan is posted on the tier-2 board. Four actions close in two weeks. One is escalated when the supplier misses the fixture delivery date — the escalation is logged the day it slips, not a week later. The sixth triggers a standard work update that is posted and verified before the plan is closed.
3 minutes to set up Status Tracker
  • Post or share the plan where the team meets (tier board, shared doc, or team page)
  • Set the review cadence before leaving this step (daily, weekly, or at milestone)
  • Escalate any item that slips its due date the day it slips, not later

FAQ

Common questions

Can two people own an action? +

No. Shared ownership reliably produces no ownership. When two people share an action, each assumes the other is handling it. Name the one person who will be asked at the next review whether the action is done. That is the owner. If the work genuinely requires two people, split the action into two: one per owner, each with its own due date.

How often should we review the plan? +

Match the cadence to the velocity of the work. Daily stand-up actions should be reviewed at the next stand-up. Kaizen follow-through actions should be reviewed at least weekly, at the tier-2 board. Project actions with milestones longer than a week should have a weekly status check. The rule of thumb: if an action can go two days without anyone noticing it slipped, your cadence is too slow.

What makes a good action? +

A good action has three properties. First, it starts with a verb and names the deliverable: "Update the setup checklist," not "Setup checklist." Second, it has a clear done signal that anyone on the team can verify, not just the owner. Third, it belongs to one person on a specific date. If you cannot write those three things in one row of a table, the action needs more definition before it goes on a plan.

How is this different from a project plan? +

A project plan captures milestones, dependencies, and a schedule across weeks or months. An action plan captures the concrete steps that move you toward one outcome, each owned and dated. Most project plans need an action plan inside each milestone. Most action plans are not full project plans. Use the Improvement or Daily type when the work is a follow-through on a solved problem or a stand-up capture. Use the Project type when you have cross-functional milestones and dependencies that need explicit tracking across more than two or three weeks.

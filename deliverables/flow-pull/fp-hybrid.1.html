Assembly cell one-piece flow
Engine assembly · fabrication line
What good looks like
  • Batches shrink and work moves without piling up
  • The line paces to takt
  • WIP is visible and capped between stations
✦ Recommended template
Continuous Flow Design →
How to design it
  1. Identify the constraint station that sets the pace for the whole cell.
  2. Link adjacent steps so work moves one piece at a time, with no queue between them.
  3. Set WIP caps at each handoff so overflow is visible, not hidden in a buffer.
  4. Balance station times to takt; redeploy capacity from the non-constraint stations.
  5. Post the flow at the line and audit daily for signs of re-batching.
Worked example
Assembly cell · Lynn, MA
A four-station assembly cell links steps into one-piece flow and cuts WIP by half. Cycle time drops from 3.2 hours to 2.4 hours because batches no longer queue between stations.
Who owns it
Cell lead with manufacturing engineer support. Flow design is revised when takt changes or a station is rebalanced.

A pull and kanban system.

For replacing only what the customer consumed. Replenishment
Example
Stockroom min/max kanban
Parts replenishment · consumables · raw material
What good looks like
  • Inventory is capped and visible at all times
  • Replenishment is triggered by use, not a forecast
  • Stockouts drop because signals are reliable
✦ Recommended template
Kanban Sizing Worksheet →
How to design it
  1. Calculate average daily demand and replenishment lead time for each item.
  2. Set a minimum (reorder trigger) and maximum (cap) for each bin or location.
  3. Create a visual signal: empty bin, card, or board that triggers the pull.
  4. Test the signal for one week; adjust min/max if stockouts or overflow occur.
  5. Review sizing quarterly or when demand shifts by more than 20 percent.
Worked example
Stockroom · Evendale, OH
A stockroom sets min/max kanban for the 40 highest-velocity parts and removes 30 percent of held inventory within the first month. Stockouts drop from 12 per week to 2 because the signal is visual and reliable.
Who owns it
Stockroom supervisor with supply chain analyst support. Sizing is reviewed each quarter.

Pull in a transactional flow.

For office work that gets pushed in batches. Office
Example
Claims team WIP cap
Knowledge work · transactional flows · service cycles
What good looks like
  • Work in progress is limited per person and visible
  • The next item is pulled only when capacity frees
  • Lead time falls because batches stop piling up
✦ Recommended template
Transactional Pull Board →
How to design it
  1. Map the transactional flow and find where work piles up between steps.
  2. Set a WIP cap per person or per step based on realistic capacity.
  3. Create a pull board: each column is a stage; cards move left to right only when downstream is ready.
  4. Run for two weeks; track lead time and spot where work stacks against the cap.
  5. Rebalance the cap at the constraint and reduce it over time as flow improves.
Worked example
Claims team · Cincinnati, OH
A claims team caps WIP at three items per person. Within six weeks, lead time drops by a third and rework falls because reviewers are no longer context-switching across eight open cases at once.
Who owns it
Team lead with process analyst support. WIP cap is reviewed monthly and tightened as flow stabilizes.

01

Find the constraint

Before designing any flow or pull system, locate the one step that sets the pace for everything else. The constraint is where work piles up most, where output is smallest, or where delays originate. Every improvement starts here. Trying to pull from an unidentified constraint produces instability, not flow.

Worked exampleA five-station assembly cell tracks cycle times at each station for three days. Station 3 consistently runs at 2.8 hours while the others clear in 1.8 to 2.2. The constraint is Station 3. All subsequent flow design is paced to that station's output, not the fastest step.
6 minutes Constraint Finder
  • Observe or collect cycle time data at every step in the flow
  • Identify the single step where work accumulates or output is lowest
  • Confirm the constraint with the team before moving to flow design
Open the Constraint Finder
02

Connect the flow

Link the steps in your flow so work moves without batching. One-piece flow means the next step starts as soon as the previous one finishes, with no queue in between. For office or replenishment flows, this means defining who hands off to whom, what triggers the handoff, and how much can be in motion at one time.

Worked exampleThe same assembly cell removes the holding shelf between Stations 2 and 3. Parts now move one at a time. Average WIP at that handoff drops from 14 pieces to 1. The team sets a physical cap of 2 pieces maximum between any two stations so flow stays visible.
8 minutes Flow Design
  • Remove or limit buffers between steps so work cannot accumulate unseen
  • Define the handoff trigger between every adjacent step
  • Set a physical or visual WIP cap at each handoff point
Open the Flow Design Template
03

Set the pull signal

A pull signal tells upstream to produce only what downstream consumed. Size the signal based on demand rate, replenishment lead time, and the safety stock you need to cover variation. Too large and you are pushing; too small and you create stockouts. The goal is the smallest amount needed to keep the flow moving reliably.

Worked exampleA stockroom item has average daily demand of 8 units and a 2-day replenishment lead time. Minimum (reorder point) is set at 16 units plus a safety stock of 4, giving a trigger at 20 units. Maximum is set at 40 units, capping the inventory position. The team marks the bin clearly so anyone can see the signal without counting.
8 minutes Pull Sizing
  • Calculate demand rate and replenishment lead time for each item or step
  • Set the reorder point (minimum) and the inventory cap (maximum)
  • Make the signal physical and visible so it cannot be missed
Open the Pull Sizing Worksheet
04

Stabilize and watch

A pull system is only stable if the signals are visible and someone is watching them. Make the board or bin state visible to the whole team. Watch for two failure modes: stockout (signal triggered too late or ignored) and overflow (cap breached without action). Adjust the sizing when demand shifts or lead time changes, not before.

Worked exampleThe assembly cell posts the pull board at the daily tier meeting. For the first two weeks, the team tracks every stockout and overflow event. Three adjustments are made to the sizing. After week three, no stockouts occur and the board requires less than five minutes of daily attention to manage.
4 minutes Pull Control Board
  • Post the pull board or bin state where the whole team can see it
  • Track stockout and overflow events for the first two weeks
  • Adjust sizing only after seeing two or more events of the same type

FAQ

Common questions

What is the difference between push and pull? +

Push means producing based on a schedule or forecast regardless of what downstream actually needs. Pull means producing only what downstream consumed, triggered by a signal from the customer or the next step. Push systems build inventory because the schedule is never perfectly accurate. Pull systems cap inventory because nothing is produced without a signal. Most operations are a mix; the goal is to increase the proportion of pull wherever demand is visible enough to signal.

What is a kanban? +

Kanban is a visual signal that authorizes production or replenishment. The signal can be a card, an empty bin, a marked floor location, or a digital flag. The key property is that nothing is produced or moved without the signal. Kanban sizing sets the minimum (reorder point) and maximum (cap) for each item. When inventory drops to the minimum, the signal triggers replenishment up to the maximum. The math is simple: minimum equals demand during replenishment lead time plus safety stock. Maximum equals minimum plus one replenishment quantity.

Do we need flow before pull? +

Stable flow makes pull more reliable, but you do not need perfect flow to start. Many teams implement a basic pull signal while flow is still being improved. The sequence that works best is: identify the constraint, stabilize it, then connect flow around it, then size the pull signal. Trying to set kanban quantities when flow is highly variable produces unreliable signals. The practical test is whether you can predict demand well enough to calculate a reorder point. If yes, start with pull. If demand is too erratic, stabilize the process first.

Does pull work in an office? +

Yes. Transactional pull is the same principle applied to information work. Instead of a bin, the signal is a WIP cap: each person or step can hold only a defined number of items in progress. When an item is completed and passed downstream, the next item is pulled in. The pull board makes the cap and the state of each item visible. The result is shorter lead time because batches stop accumulating and context switching falls. Claims processing, engineering reviews, contract approvals, and close cycles have all benefited from transactional pull boards.

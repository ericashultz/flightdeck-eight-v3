Do you run the daily?

For team leaders and supervisors who lead the stand-up Run the board
Recommended template
Tier 1 Daily Management Board
Cell lead · Supervisor · Team leader
What good looks like
  • The board drives the conversation, not the other way around
  • Every miss has an owner before the stand-up ends
  • Escalations are timely and documented at the board
✦ Recommended template
Tier 1 Daily Management Board →
How to run it
  1. Start at the board on time. The board is the agenda.
  2. Review SQDC top to bottom. Green means on target, any other color means explain why.
  3. Name an owner for every item that is not green before moving on.
  4. Park problems that need more than two minutes on the problems list.
  5. Close in ten minutes. Escalate anything that cannot be resolved at tier 1.
Worked example
Cell lead · Tier 1 SQDC stand-up
A cell lead runs a ten-minute SQDC stand-up each morning at the board. Every metric is updated before the team arrives. Safety is green. Quality shows one miss from yesterday: the owner is named, a one-line note is on the board, and the stand-up moves on.
Who owns it
The team leader or supervisor. Coaching support from the value-stream manager.

Do you update the board?

For operators and frontline staff who keep the board current Update the board
Recommended template
SQDC Tracking Sheet
Operator · Frontline staff · Cell member
What good looks like
  • The board is updated in real time, not at the end of the shift
  • Red is honest: problems are written down, not hidden
  • One-line problem notes give the team leader enough context to act
✦ Recommended template
SQDC Tracking Sheet →
How to update it
  1. Mark the metric as soon as the result is known. Do not wait until the stand-up.
  2. Use the agreed color convention: green is on target, any miss is marked accordingly.
  3. Write a one-line problem note for any miss. Who noticed it, what happened.
  4. Do not erase or change a miss before the stand-up. The board is the record.
  5. If you need help, flag the team leader at the board before the meeting, not after.
Worked example
Operator · Quality miss at the cell
An operator catches a quality deviation on the second unit of the shift. They mark the Quality metric and add a one-line note: "Unit 2 thread gauge out of spec, quarantined at station 3." The board is updated before the stand-up. The team leader has what they need to act.
Who owns it
The operator or designated cell member for each shift. Not delegated to the team leader.

What good looks like
  • Tiers connect: a tier-1 miss that repeats surfaces at tier-2 within 48 hours
  • Repeat problems route to a root-cause owner, not back to the operator
  • The board is tied to the metric that matters at that tier
✦ Recommended template
Tiered Escalation Matrix →
How to improve it
  1. Map the current tiers: what boards exist, who runs them, how they connect.
  2. Define the escalation trigger: what condition at tier 1 routes a problem to tier 2.
  3. Name the tier-2 and tier-3 review owners before the first escalation happens.
  4. Audit the board for metric alignment: does the tier-1 metric tie to the tier-2 target?
  5. Review the escalation log monthly; close root-cause actions at the right tier.
Worked example
Engineer · Tier escalation from cell to value stream
A recurring tier-1 quality miss on the same part number appears three days running. The engineer reviews the escalation matrix, confirms the trigger condition is met, and routes the problem to the tier-2 value-stream review. A root-cause owner is named within the same day. The tier-1 board is updated to show escalated status.
Who owns it
Lead engineer or continuous-improvement manager. Escalation paths defined before they are needed.

01

Choose the tier and metrics

Before anything goes on the board, the team needs to agree on the tier and the four to six metrics that matter at that level. Pick the tier first: tier 1 is the cell or team, tier 2 is the value stream, tier 3 is the site or business unit. Then choose the SQDC metrics that reflect what the team is accountable for.

Worked exampleA team picks tier 1 with Safety, Quality, Delivery, and Cost as their daily metrics. Safety is measured by near-miss reports. Quality is first-pass yield. Delivery is units to schedule. Cost is overtime hours. Four metrics, each with a clear daily target.
5 minutes SQDC Board Template
  • Confirm the tier: 1 (cell), 2 (value stream), or 3 (site)
  • Name four to six SQDC metrics with a daily target for each
  • Confirm these are metrics the team controls, not just measures they watch
Open the SQDC Board Template
02

Build the board

Lay out the visual board where the team gathers every day. Physical boards work best when the team shares a space. Digital boards work when the team is distributed. Either way, the board needs to be visible to every member, updated before the stand-up, and simple enough to read in under 30 seconds.

Worked exampleA physical board is mounted at the cell entrance. It has four SQDC columns, today's date, a target row and an actual row, a color zone, and a problems parking lot at the bottom. All fields are pre-printed. The team fills it in daily with a marker. Nothing is typed or digitized at this stage.
8 minutes Tier Board Layout
  • Place the board where the stand-up will happen, not in a conference room
  • Include a target row, an actual row, a color zone, and a problems area
  • Confirm every team member can update it without asking for help
Open the Tier Board Layout
03

Run the daily stand-up

Set the time, the standing agenda, and the ten-minute cadence before the first stand-up. The board is the agenda. The leader facilitates. The team answers. Every miss gets an owner. Problems that take more than two minutes to discuss go to the parking lot.

Worked exampleThe stand-up is at 7:30am at the board, every day. The cell lead reviews SQDC top to bottom. Safety is green: no action. Quality shows a miss from the previous shift: the operator names the issue, the lead assigns an owner, and a note goes on the board. Delivery and Cost are green. The meeting ends in nine minutes.
3 minutes to set up Stand-up Cadence Guide
  • Set a fixed time and location and put it on the team calendar
  • Agree on the SQDC review order and the two-minute rule for parking lot items
  • Run the first stand-up today, even if the board is not perfect yet
Open the Stand-up Cadence Guide
04

Escalate and act

Define what escalates, to whom, and when before the first problem needs to go up the chain. A clear escalation path means the team does not lose time debating the question in the moment. The escalation matrix connects tier 1 to tier 2 and tier 3 so that repeat problems get the right level of attention.

Worked exampleAny item on the tier-1 problems list that is unresolved after two days automatically routes to the tier-2 value-stream review. The escalation matrix lists the trigger, the receiving owner, and the response time. A recurring delivery miss escalates within 48 hours. The tier-2 owner is named before it ever happens.
2 minutes Escalation Matrix
  • Define the escalation trigger: what condition sends an item to the next tier
  • Name the tier-2 and tier-3 owners before the first escalation is needed
  • Post the escalation path at or near the board so the whole team can see it

FAQ

Common questions

Physical board or digital? +

Physical boards are faster to update, easier to see at a glance, and require no login or load time. They are the default for shop-floor teams who share a space. Digital boards work well for distributed teams or when the board needs to feed a tier-2 or tier-3 review automatically. Start physical if you are new to daily management. You can migrate to digital once the habit is set and the metrics are stable.

What metrics go on a tier 1 board? +

Tier 1 boards track the four to six SQDC metrics the team directly controls: Safety (near misses, incidents), Quality (first-pass yield, defects), Delivery (units to schedule, on-time shipment), and Cost (overtime, scrap rate). Every metric should have a daily target and a result the team can update themselves. Avoid metrics that require a manager to retrieve the data. If the team cannot update it in real time, it does not belong on a tier-1 board.

How long should the stand-up be? +

Ten minutes. Review SQDC top to bottom: green items get a nod, any non-green item gets a named owner and a one-line note. Problems that need more than two minutes to discuss go to the parking lot and are addressed after the stand-up, not during it. If your stand-ups regularly run past ten minutes, the board is probably doing too much work that should happen before or after the meeting.

What is the difference between tier 1, 2, and 3? +

Tier 1 is the team or cell level: daily stand-up, SQDC metrics, problems resolved by the team lead within the shift. Tier 2 is the value-stream or department level: issues escalated from tier 1 that need cross-team ownership or more than a day to resolve. Tier 3 is the site or business-unit level: systemic problems, capacity constraints, or anything that requires executive or cross-site action. The tiers are connected: an unresolved tier-1 miss should reach tier 2 within 48 hours, and an unresolved tier-2 issue should reach tier 3 within the week.

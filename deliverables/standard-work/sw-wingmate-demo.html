Start a Standard

Your first standard, step by step

First, pick your world below so the steps fit — every example and template matches your work, and you can switch anytime. Each step then has a short action, a worked example, the template it points to, and a small checklist.

Pick Manufacturing, Non-Manufacturing, or Leader above to reveal your steps.

01

Go to Genba and grasp the work as-is

Get to where the work happens. Introduce yourself to the operator, watch about ten minutes before you start timing, and understand the content and sequence of the current best-known method. Grasp reality with facts, not data — don’t rely on automated reports for operator cycle times, and don’t train the observer.

Eliminate waste firstBefore you document, reduce waste (DOWNTIME), unevenness (Mura), and overburden (Muri). If you standardize the mess, you standardize the waste.

Before you leave Genba

  • Introduced myself to the operator and explained why I’m observing.
  • Observed roughly 10 minutes before timing anything.
  • Noted the elements, hand motions, and any waste / safety concerns.
  • Confirmed 5S is in place enough to see normal vs. abnormal.
02

Calculate Takt Time and Target Cycle Time

Set the pace before you set the sequence. Takt Time is the rate the customer requires; Target Cycle Time adjusts Takt for planned losses — machine OEE (changeover, scrap, downtime) and manual yield (operator, inspection, rework) — so the standard is achievable in the real world.

Worked example 480 min shift − 20 min breaks − 30 min lunch = 430 min net.
430 min × 60 ÷ 800 parts demand = 32.3 s Takt Time.
32.3 s × 90% combined planned effectiveness ≈ 29.0 s Target Cycle Time.
Keep Takt and Cycle Time separateTakt is calculated from demand. Cycle Time is actual time taken. The goal: Cycle Time ≤ Takt.
Calculate Takt and Target Cycle Time
03

Time-study the work using the Time Measurement Form

Break the operator’s work into logical elements (verb + noun) of reasonable duration. Time 7–10 cycles with a running stopwatch, exclude irregular processes, and best done in pairs — one times, one records.

For each element, pick the lowest repeatable time — the lowest elapsed time that has occurred more than once. Do not use the average. Sum the elements to derive one cycle time you can defend at Genba.

Time-study rules

  • Broke the work into verb/noun elements of reasonable size.
  • Timed 7–10 clean cycles, excluding irregular events.
  • Used lowest repeatable time per element — not the average.
  • Two people: one timed, one recorded.
Open the Time Measurement Form
04

Document Content, Sequence, Timing — and SWIP

Your standard isn’t complete until all three elements are captured — Content (what to do), Sequence (the order), and Timing (how long it takes). In manufacturing, also set SWIP (Standard Work In-Process): the minimum quantity of parts in the process that keeps one-piece flow.

Use the six Operations templates in the order the deck recommends. Start with templates 1, 2, 3, and 6. Add templates 4 and 5 three to six months later as the team matures.

Templates to build now

01 · Time Measurement Form

As-is duration of steps, over 7–10 reps. Lowest repeatable time.

02 · Machine Capacity Sheet

Availability × Performance × Quality. Sets Planned OEE and finds excess capacity.

03 · Balance Chart (Yamazumi)

Stacked load per operator vs. Takt; splits VA / NVA-necessary / Waste to expose kaizen.

06 · Standard Work Sheet

Operator movement, SWIP quantity/location, layout, quality checks, safety precautions.

Add later (3–6 months)

04 · Standard Work Combination Sheet (SWCS)

One operator’s Manual / Auto / Walk / Waiting time against Takt.

05 · Process Scroll

Layout view: elements, Takt, output, resource needs, material flow, bottlenecks.

Leave specs and tolerances in the QMSStandard Work supports the QMS, it doesn’t duplicate it. Keep drawings, specs, and tolerances in the controlled quality docs; red-line the standard when it changes and update the QMS-side docs to match.

05

Make it visible, train the team, then follow and improve it

A standard that lives in a folder isn’t a standard. Post the Balance Chart and Standard Work Sheet where the work is performed. A good standard can be trained in under 15 minutes and makes normal vs. abnormal obvious at a glance.

Build a Daily Management go-and-see rhythm around it — a Genba walk, not an audit. When a KPI goes red, confirm the standard is being followed first, then run Problem Solving. When a better way is found, revise the standard and retrain. That’s the never-ending loop.

Sustainment checklist

  • Balance Chart and Standard Work Sheet posted at Genba.
  • Training schedule covers all shifts; each operator can be trained in < 15 min.
  • Daily Management routine includes go-and-see against the standard.
  • Balance Charts planned for different mixes / Takt scenarios.
  • QMS documents red-lined and re-signed where the standard changed anything controlled.

The four confirmation questions

  1. 1 Is there a standard?
  2. 2 Is the standard up to date?
  3. 3 Do the employees know the standard?
  4. 4 Is work carried out according to the standard?

All four should be “yes” for the standard to be leading you toward True North.

01

Start with the four questions

Standard Work documents and visualizes the best-known way to complete a task or process today. It is not a set-and-forget document; it is the starting point for your next improvement. Before you build your first standard, get oriented around the four questions every standard has to answer. You will return to these to check your work — for now, just know what good looks like.

The four key questions

  1. 1 Is there a standard?
  2. 2 Is it up to date?
  3. 3 Does the team know it?
  4. 4 Is it being followed?

If the answer to any of these is "no," you have a Standard Work opportunity. The steps that follow help you build it.

02

Determine the right level of detail

Before you document anything, decide how granular it needs to be. Standard Work operates at three levels: Process (high level), Key Steps (intermediary), and Activities (detailed / DJI). In non-manufacturing or office environments, the principle is the same: making ‘invisible’ digital work visible through clear, documented standards that the team can follow and improve. Set the level of detail to match the work, and go deep only where exact detail prevents errors.

Levels of Detail

1
Processhigh level
2
Key Stepsintermediary
3
Activitiesdetailed / DJI
Worked example The analyst maps the month-end close at the Key Steps level so anyone in the cycle can follow it, then drops to the Activities level only for the reconciliation steps where a missed detail causes rework.
  • Pick the level of detail for each part: Process, Key Steps, or Activities
  • Drop to Activities (detailed / DJI) only where exact detail prevents errors
  • Keep the Process view high-level so anyone can step in and understand it

03

Select the right template

With the level of detail set, select the template that fits your work. Office, service, or engineering work uses Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs), Master Instructions (MI), and Detailed Job Instructions (DJI). As you fill it in, apply the three elements of effective Standard Work — together they define how fast the work must go, in what order, and with how much in process at any time.

Apply the three elements

Timing

Do you know the rate at which your customer expects output? How closely does your current pace match that demand?

Work Sequence

Is the order of tasks in your process clearly defined? Does everyone follow the same sequence?

Content

Do you have the right amount of work-in-process to maintain flow? Is there too much, or too little?

  • Match the template to your work: SOP, MI, or DJI
  • Timing: confirm the customer rate and how your pace compares
  • Work Sequence: define the order so everyone follows the same one
  • Content: set the work-in-process needed to keep flow — not too much, not too little

04

Generate the first draft

Now generate the first draft of your standard work. Standard Work provides the structure for consistent, repeatable outcomes — the format may differ by environment, but the purpose is the same. Open the Non-Manufacturing template and capture the best-known way today: the sequence, the hand-offs, the owners, and the time for each step. Aim for a draft, not a final.

Worked example The analyst opens the Non-Manufacturing Standard Work template and captures each hand-off, owner, and target time across the close cycle. A draft in eight minutes. She does not wait for it to be perfect.
  • Open the template for your environment and start the draft
  • Capture the sequence and hand-offs in the real order they happen
  • Record the time per step and flag any control- or quality-critical points
  • Save it as a draft to refine, not a finished document
Open the Non-Manufacturing Standard Work Template
05

Release & Sustain It

Release the standard through the FLIGHT DECK Release Process, then keep it alive. Roles: use a RACI to name who is Responsible and Accountable for creating, training, and sustaining it. Repository: store it where the team can reach it, centralized and accessible. Sustainment: review it on a 30-60-90 day rhythm and fold it into your Daily and Visual Management routines so adherence — and any deviation — stays visible.

Worked example The analyst names who is Responsible and Accountable, stores the standard on the team page, and sets a monthly review after each close that uses the standard as its agenda. After a quarter the standard has been updated twice.
  • Roles: assign who is Responsible and Accountable with a RACI
  • Repository: store it where the team can reach it
  • Sustainment: set a 30-60-90 rhythm and add it to Daily & Visual Management
  • Re-check the four questions: standard, up to date, known, followed

01

Pick the Routine

A leader's standard is a routine, not a task. Name the single recurring routine you want to standardize — the daily Genba walk, the tier board review, the weekly one-on-one — and the best-known way you run it today. One routine, one owner, the real cadence.

Worked example A cell leader wants to standardize the daily tier-one review: "Lead the 8:30 board review." They note the cadence it must keep — every day, before the shift settles — so the routine is sized to how the team actually runs. One routine, one owner, current best practice only.
  • Name the routine in one sentence (verb first, e.g. "Lead," "Review," "Walk")
  • Confirm you are the owner of this routine right now
  • Confirm this is how you really run it today, not the ideal
  • Note the cadence the routine must keep — daily, weekly, or monthly

02

Analyze how you spend your time

Analyze how you currently spend your time. Before building a future-state routine, review your calendar for the last eight weeks so you can see where the time actually goes and decide what to free up. This is the baseline your standard routine improves on.

Worked example A cell leader exports eight weeks of calendar, tags each block (Genba, meetings, email, firefighting), and finds nine hours a week of low-value meetings to reclaim for tier reviews and coaching.
  • Pull your calendar for the last eight weeks
  • Tag each block by type so you can see where the time goes
  • Identify the low-value time to free up for the routine
Open the Calendar Analysis
03

Schedule and Execute It Consistently

Put the routine on the calendar and post it where the team can see the leader's cadence. A Calendar Analysis shows where your time actually goes; a recurring scheduler protects the routine. Visualizing it makes the leader's standard work as visible as the team's.

Worked example The leader runs a Calendar Analysis, blocks the tier reviews on a recurring scheduler, and posts the LSW on the team board so everyone knows when the leader checks what. One placement at the Genba, zero extra tools.
  • Post the LSW at the Genba — the team board where work happens
  • Block the routine on a recurring calendar or scheduler
  • Run a Calendar Analysis to protect the time from drift

04

Sustain it

Maximize how you spend your time. A leader's routine is never finished; it is the starting point for the next improvement. Follow the Release Process: name who covers the routine when you are out (RACI), keep the LSW in a repository the team can reach, and review it on a 30-60-90 day rhythm so adherence — and any deviation — stays visible.

Worked example The leader names who runs the routine when they are out, sets a monthly review of their own LSW, and uses it as the agenda for that review. After a quarter the routine has been tightened twice and the coverage plan has held.
  • Assign who is Responsible and Accountable (RACI), including a backup
  • Set a 30-60-90 day review rhythm for your own LSW
  • Check it against the four questions: is there a standard, is it up to date, does the team know it, is it being followed?

Common questions

Common questions

My work is not repetitive. Does standard work apply? +

Yes. Standard work applies to any task that repeats at least once. Even knowledge work has sequences: how you structure a report, how you run a design review, how you hand off a drawing. You are not locking in a rigid script. You are capturing the best known approach so you have a baseline to improve from. The standard changes when you find a better way.

How detailed does the standard need to be? +

Detailed enough that someone new could follow it on day one without asking questions. That is the test. Most first drafts are too long. Aim for the steps that actually affect quality, safety, or timing. Everything else can live in a supporting note or link.

What is the difference between Manufacturing SW, Non-Manufacturing SW, and Leader SW? +

Manufacturing Standard Work captures physical steps at a workstation with cycle times and takt time. Non-Manufacturing Standard Work captures sequences for office, service, or engineering tasks where the work is information-based, not physical. Leader Standard Work captures the daily and weekly routines a team leader follows to observe, develop, and sustain a system. If you are not on the shop floor, start with Non-Manufacturing.

How often should I update a standard? +

Update it whenever the process changes, whenever you find a better way, or whenever training someone reveals a gap. For stable processes, a monthly review is enough. A standard that has not been reviewed in six months is almost certainly out of date.

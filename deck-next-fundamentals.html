01 · Standard Work

Set the pace
before the sequence.

A live Takt Time & Target Cycle Time calculator, one click from Standard Work step 2. Enter shift, breaks, and customer demand — it returns Takt Time and, after planned losses (machine OEE and manual yield), the Target Cycle Time to design the standard to.

Worked example, live
  • Net available time. Gross shift minus breaks, lunch, and planned stops.
  • Takt and Target. Net time ÷ demand gives Takt; Takt × combined planned losses (machine OEE × manual yield) gives the achievable Target Cycle Time.
  • Operators required. Total workload ÷ Takt, rounded up — checked against your measured cycle time.

01 · Standard Work · View 1 of 3

The Manufacturing
view.

Pick your world drives the whole build path. Choose Manufacturing and every step, example, and template fits shop-floor work paced to cycle time and takt.

The five steps, tuned to the floor ↓
1Go to Genba and grasp the work as-is. Watch the work where it happens and capture the current best-known method with facts, not data — and reduce waste, Mura, and Muri before you standardize it.
2Calculate Takt Time and Target Cycle Time. Set the pace before the sequence: Takt is the rate the customer requires; Target Cycle Time adjusts it for planned losses so the standard is achievable.
3Time-study the work. Logical elements (verb + noun), timed over 7–10 cycles with a running stopwatch — best done in pairs.
4Document Content, Sequence, Timing — and SWIP. The standard isn't complete until all three elements are captured, plus the standard work in-process that keeps the cell flowing.
5Make it visible, train the team, then follow and improve it. Balance Chart and Standard Work Sheet posted at the work — a good standard trains in under 15 minutes.

05 · Operating Cadences · View 1 of 3

The Daily cadence.

Tier 1, at the cell. The Daily Management System is the ten-minute loop with the week — leading metrics that reveal problems and remove roadblocks while they’re small.

The four steps, at the daily tier ↓
1Pick the cadence and tier. A daily tier-1 review lives at the cell level, with one named owner — pick one cadence and stand it up properly.
2Set the standing agenda. Two minutes on SQDC metrics, misses to owners, the action log closes the meeting — a draft you tighten after the first cycles.
3Run it on time. Start even if the room isn’t full. Misses are called against the board, not opinions — every action leaves with one owner and one date.
4Close the loop. Most daily actions close same-day; recurring misses are the signal to escalate up to the weekly review.

The Weekly cadence.

Tier 2, at the value stream. The Weekly Operating Review reads leading metrics tied to process performance — learn, remove barriers, launch problem solving.

The four steps, at the weekly tier ↓
1Pick the cadence and tier. A weekly tier-2 review sits at the value stream — replacing metrics discussed ad hoc with actions that fall through.
2Set the standing agenda. Five minutes on open actions, then the board drives the discussion — attendees arrive knowing the order.
3Run it on time. Forty-five minutes, protected. Eight of ten open actions close in the meeting; nothing enters the log without an owner and a date.
4Close the loop. Audit the log after four weeks — recurring slips escalate to the monthly review on a path defined before you need it.

The Monthly cadence.

Tier 3, at the site. The Monthly Operating Review and Hoshin Kanri Review manage lagging KPIs tied to outcomes — and the strategic breakthroughs behind them.

The four steps, at the monthly tier ↓
1Pick the cadence and tier. A monthly tier-3 review belongs to the site or business unit — rebuilt for decisions, not status.

The Manufacturing
stream.

Door-to-door production flow: materials, machines, and takt. PQPR starts with the family — pick your stream, then run one turn of the VSA cycle against it.

The four steps, walked on the floor ↓
1Map the current state. Walk the line door to door and record every step, cycle time, and the inventory between steps — what actually happens, not what should.
2Find the waste. Mark the queues, the rework loops, and the batch sizes — then find the constraint that paces the whole stream.
3Design the future state. Attack the constraint and the largest single waste first — a pull signal at the feeding process beats a redesign that never ships.
4Plan and manage to it. Each gap becomes a kaizen burst with a named owner, a target date, and a measurable result — reviewed monthly at minimum.

Create flow.

Phase 2 of the journey. Choose families that work together and implement one-piece flow — the four steps read as a production cell removing batching from the line.

The four steps, on the line ↓
1Find the constraint. Three days of cycle-time data finds the station pacing the cell — every improvement starts there, not at the loudest complaint.
2Connect the flow. Remove the holding shelf between stations — parts move one at a time and WIP falls with them.
3Set the pull signal. Takt from available time and demand — the signal sized so upstream produces only what downstream consumed.
4Stabilize and watch. The pull board reads at the daily tier meeting — stockout and overflow events tracked and acted on for the first weeks.

08 · Flow & Pull · View 2 of 3

Incorporate pull.

Phase 3. Where you can’t combine processes, implement standardized pull — the kanban system, sized from real demand instead of guesses.

The four steps, as replenishment ↓
1Find the constraint. Map the highest-velocity parts and 30 days of daily demand data before sizing anything.
2Connect the flow. A dedicated bin with a clear min/max label per item — the empty bin is the visible hand-off trigger.
3Set the pull signal. Reorder point = demand over lead time plus safety stock — too large and you’re pushing, too small and you stock out.
4Stabilize and watch. Repeated stockouts raise the reorder point; overflow lowers the cap — the system tunes itself within weeks.

Level load &
linearity.

Phase 4. Level load every step and cap transactional work — a pull board with WIP limits makes invisible office work flow like a line.

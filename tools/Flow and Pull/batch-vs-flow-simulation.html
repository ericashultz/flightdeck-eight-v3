The scenario

A customer submits an order for 10 parts. Every part must pass through three stations — Station A, Station B, and Station C — in that order. Each station needs exactly 1 minute per part, no matter how the work is organized.

Both runs start with all 10 items sitting in Raw Materials — nothing is pre-staged at a station. Press Play and watch where they go.

  • Run 1 — Batch & Queue: each station finishes all 10 parts, and the whole batch waits in a queue, before the next station can start.

  • Run 2 — Continuous Flow: each part moves the instant it's done — one piece at a time, with virtually no queue.

Both runs have a "defect" switch — look for the amber toggle in the controls bar. Flip it on to see what happens when Part #3 comes out of Station A with a hidden flaw, and how long it takes each system to notice.

Only Handle It Once

In batch & queue, every item gets set down, queued, and picked back up again — and again. In flow, each item is touched once per step and handed straight to the next person. Every extra pick-up is time and motion that never should have happened.

True North

One-by-one flow to customer takt

Single-piece flow is the ideal — but you don't have to jump there in one move. Cutting a 500-piece batch to 250, then 100, then 20 captures most of the same lead-time and WIP benefit at every step down.

Takeaways to carry back to work

Lead time isn't about how fast people work — it's about how long items wait. Every step in both runs worked at exactly the same pace. The difference was queue time between steps.

Small WIP limits are a feature, not a constraint. Flow needed 3 parts in the system at once instead of 10 — less space, less handling, less to lose track of.

Flow catches problems while they're still cheap. The same defect cost one part and one minute in flow, and threatened the entire order in batch & queue — because it sat hidden inside a queue for over 30 minutes before anyone looked at it again.

This is the "fake flow" test. If a process looks smooth end-to-end but work still piles up and waits between steps, you're getting the batch outcome dressed up to look like flow — whether that's a shop floor cell or an approval workflow.
